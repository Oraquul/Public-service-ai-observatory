What should happen when an AI assistant at a public-service desk is asked to approve an application? In this worked rehearsal, it stops. It sends the enquiry to an officer and records the reason. That boundary is as important as the draft it can produce.

The example imagines a Nigerian export-enquiry desk helping first-time exporters and small businesses find reliable guidance. NEPC's public e-registration page supplies a relevant context for guidance and assistance. This is an independent fictional design. It is not a report of an NEPC implementation, a claim about the Council's performance or an institutional endorsement.

## The service problem

The service problem is familiar: an officer must understand a question, find the right guidance, prepare a clear response and decide who has authority to act. Some enquiries are routine. Others involve missing information, a payment, a certificate or destination-specific requirements. The proposed assistant can help prepare a draft. It cannot decide those matters.

Users in the fiction are first-time exporters, small businesses and desk officers. The intended value is accurate guidance, less repetitive preparation and a clear escalation route. Export growth is a long-term hypothesis outside this case's measurement scope. No volume, backlog or service failure is attributed to any institution. The desk in the design must still offer a human and low-bandwidth route. The rehearsal is in English only.

## Orchestration

The orchestration joins eight functions: intake, privacy filtering, triage, retrieval, drafting, validation, officer review and audit recording. Current approved sources are required before drafting. The officer remains responsible for the response. A missing source or a timeout sends the item to a manual queue. A confident tone does not substitute for authority.

The proposed steps, and the boundary at each one, are:

1. Intake validates the enquiry and creates a case identifier. Attachments and credentials stay out of the drafting path.
2. A privacy filter separates contact details from the question text. This rehearsal recognises synthetic markers only. Real free-text redaction is untested.
3. Triage assigns routine, ambiguous, out-of-scope or complex. In the rehearsal code the category is an input fixture, not an AI classification.
4. Retrieval selects current, approved knowledge-base records. Official source text is evidence, not a set of instructions the tool may follow.
5. Drafting prepares an answer and pairs claims with sources. Here the draft is a handcrafted fixture.
6. A validator rejects unsupported guarantees, stale sources and actions beyond the desk's authority.
7. A desk officer checks every draft, corrects claims and decides whether to send or escalate. The assistant has no payment, certificate, approval or procurement tool.
8. An audit record stores the minimum authorised metadata, versions, reason codes, labour, costs and the review decision. It leaves unnecessary personal data out of any editorial export.

Routine output is a draft, not an autonomous reply. Fees, payment status, certificate issuance, an ambiguous entity and destination-specific requirements are escalated. Use a rules route plus a drafting component before considering several autonomous agents.

## The constructed comparison

We constructed sixty question IDs and repeated them across three approaches: manual handling, a searchable knowledge base, and AI-assisted drafting with human review. The mix included thirty routine, twelve ambiguous, eight out-of-scope and ten complex questions. All handling times and correctness outcomes were assigned as test fixtures. No model was called; no citizens or officers were observed. The exercise tests the reporting workflow and arithmetic, not real AI performance.

In this constructed cohort, manual handling uses 985 active labour minutes, the searchable knowledge base uses 653, and AI-assisted work uses 546. Final correct answers are 55, 57 and 58 out of sixty respectively. AI drafts initially satisfy the fixture in 52 cases; six simulated corrections bring the final count to 58. Two non-critical omissions remain. The AI arm includes 24 correction minutes, rather than treating review as free.

The comparison teaches an uncomfortable but useful lesson. Better information management can create much of the apparent benefit attributed to AI. Against manual handling, the AI arm releases 439 assumed labour minutes. Against the searchable knowledge base, it releases 107 minutes. Neither number is an observed productivity gain or a cash saving.

Manual handling completes 28 of 30 required escalations and keeps one critical final error in the fixture. The knowledge base completes 29 of 30 escalations and has no critical final error. The AI arm completes 30 of 30 escalations and has no critical final error after review. A zero critical-error count in a constructed fixture is not evidence of safety in a deployment. Correctness here means only that the final answer met the fixture's expected guidance and route.

## Cost under stated assumptions

Using an illustrative labour value of NGN3,000 an hour, AI usage of NGN60 per enquiry and incremental AI operations of NGN5,000 for the cohort, the in-scope totals are NGN49,250 for manual handling, NGN32,650 for the knowledge base and NGN35,900 for AI. Shared platform setup and knowledge-base creation costs are omitted for all approaches. This is not a total ownership budget. AI is faster in the fixture but costs NGN3,250 more than the knowledge-base alternative under these assumptions.

The non-labour AI amount in the fixture is NGN8,600, made up of NGN3,600 in per-enquiry usage and NGN5,000 in operations. Dividing that amount by the 107 minutes saved against the knowledge base gives a cohort break-even of NGN4,822.43 an hour, before the omitted setup costs. That figure is a sensitivity calculation. It is not a recommended wage. Released time is potential capacity, not a cash saving and not an export-growth effect.

## What review blocked

The rehearsal also follows a failure. Draft v1 describes AI as the cheapest option and includes an unsupported approval guarantee in a service fixture. Review blocks both claims. The editor corrects the cost interpretation; the desk boundary blocks the guarantee. Dependent video and newsletter assets are marked stale until their claims are updated and reapproved.

Draft v1 was not published on this site. The text above is draft v2.

## What the local checks do not show

Fifteen local software assertions pass, including stale-source blocking, a synthetic-marker redaction, scope escalation, timeout fallback, permission checks, approval hashes and a duplicate-write recovery in a mock publisher. Those are narrow checks. They do not establish that a real model resists adversarial instructions, that a production system protects personal data, or that people can use it successfully.

The checks do not test a live model, a browser, a data connector, real redaction, citizen usability, cyber security or a publishing provider. The script is deterministic and calls no external service. A mock publication writes only to an in-memory record.

## What the Think Tank should do next

What should the Think Tank do next? Review the answer criteria, source ownership, officer workload and non-AI alternative. A prospective host could then seek authority for a controlled shadow pilot using actual de-identified tasks, independently scored answers and full costs. No citizen should receive an autonomous response from this rehearsal.

The Observatory's outcome is therefore a transparent E0 case, a replication pack and a proposal for a real evaluation. It is not a success badge. Our job is to make the question, evidence, failure and next decision clear enough for another public-service leader to challenge and adapt.

## Three enquiry traces

These traces are role plays. No officer carried them out, and the routine reply is illustrative text, not an officially approved response.

**SIM-001, routine.** A first-time exporter asks where to start. Intake assigns an identifier, a mock approved guide is retrieved, and a source-linked draft enters officer review. The mock final response is: “Start with the public registration guidance at https://nepc.gov.ng/get-started/e-registration/. For your organisation's specific requirements, contact the relevant official assistance channel. This desk cannot approve an application or verify a payment.”

**SIM-037, ambiguous.** The entity type is missing. The draft suggests a fictional unsupported fee marker, SIM-FEE-999. The claim check blocks it. The officer in the role play removes the fee and asks for the entity type through the authorised channel. Nothing is released automatically.

**SIM-044, outside scope.** A requester asks for certificate approval and payment confirmation. A bad draft promises guaranteed approval. The service boundary blocks it and routes it to an officer. No credentials are requested. INC-002 records the near miss. If a real unsafe answer had been released, the affected route would stop, the service owner would be contacted, and the incident would follow the institution's own process. That process was not invoked here, because nothing was released to a real user.

## Limitations

- No citizen, officer or model was observed. The sixty questions are not a representative sample or a held-out benchmark.
- The privacy test redacts a synthetic marker. It does not show that real free text would be protected.
- The fifteen assertions do not show adversarial robustness, production security or that people can use the service.
- The illustrative score of 80 out of 100 does not certify readiness. Evidence integrity in that score means honest disclosure of synthetic evidence.
- The case is in English only. Accessibility, assisted completion and other languages were not tested with intended users.
- Time released is not a budget saving. The cost totals are not a total cost of ownership.
- Public institution permissions remain unconfirmed. The case cannot be listed as a successful deployment or as an independently replicated result.
- Rerunning the local code does not raise the evidence level above E0.

## Replication pack

REP-001 is a proposed adaptation. No host outcome is recorded. A prospective host would need a named service owner, a lawful data and authority review, hosting approval, an incident route, a current approved source owner, reviewer time and an evaluation protocol. Blog consent and formal research consent are separate. A phone or in-person route should remain available. Taxonomy, permitted actions, source refresh, languages, devices, networks, labour measures and provider costs have to be adapted locally.

The proposed six-week sequence is contingent on authority, capacity and funding. It is not a launch commitment, and its gates have not been met.

Proposed acceptance gates, none of which this rehearsal meets, include at least 95 percent final quality on an agreed rubric, no critical released error, and every required escalation completed. A small sample is a safety screen, not proof of safety. A further proposal is at least 15 percent less active labour than the knowledge-base comparator, with quality no worse, and a full-cost or justified capacity case using actual rates. An unsafe released answer would trigger the institution's incident process and a return to the manual service.

A real implementation description can support E1. Suitable real measurements may support E2. E3 requires an independently documented replication by a separate institution. See the [evidence levels](/tools/evidence-levels/) and the [decision-boundary checklist](/tools/decision-boundary/).
